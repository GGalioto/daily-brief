2. 🎓 Business School — Module 22: Hiring Your First Contractor/Subcontractor

The gap nobody warns you about: the day you pay someone else to do real work for your business, you cross from "solo operator" into "employer of labor" in the eyes of the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and your state — whether you meant to or not. Get the classification wrong and the bill isn't just back-taxes. It's back-taxes plus penalties plus the worker's own claim for benefits you never budgeted, all landing at once, usually years after the work happened.

The classification line, plainly: an employee is someone whose work you control — you say when, where, how, using what tools, and you can fire them for not following your process. A contractor controls their own how — you buy an outcome, they decide the method, they can work for other people at the same time, they often have their own tools/business/other clients. The IRS uses a "right to control" test with roughly 20 factors bucketed into three: behavioral control (do you direct the work?), financial control (do they have real business risk — their own equipment, unreimbursed expenses, ability to profit or lose?), and relationship type (is there a contract, benefits, is the work indefinite/central to your business?). No single factor decides it — it's the whole pattern.

Why this matters at Geo's exact stage: Huginn is not a person, so this doesn't apply there — but the moment you bring on a real human subcontractor (say, someone to help with the Rainbow build, or eventually a second GoHighLevel (GHL) builder), this is the first legal document you'll actually need. Misclassifying someone as a "contractor" because it's simpler than payroll is one of the most common — and most expensive — small-business mistakes; the IRS runs entire audit programs specifically hunting for it, because it's the easiest way for a business to dodge payroll tax, workers' comp, and unemployment insurance, intentionally or not.

Scope and pay structure — the two things a contract has to nail: (1) Scope — a specific deliverable with a defined end (ties straight back to Module 18's contract/scope-creep lesson: name what's included, name what's excluded, define the change-order process before work starts, not after). (2) Pay structure — project-based or milestone-based pay is the contractor-friendly default (pay-by-the-hour-on-a-schedule starts to look more like employment in the IRS's eyes). A 1099 form gets issued at year-end for anyone paid $600+ in the year; a W-9 gets collected from them BEFORE the first payment goes out, not after — this is the piece people forget and then scramble for in January.

Worked example, tied to today's Best Lane pick: imagine Rainbow says yes and volume shows up faster than you can build solo. The tempting move is "pay a buddy $30/hr to help file forms." That's exactly the pattern that gets reclassified: you'd be setting the hours, dictating the exact process (because the filing has to match your engine's schema), and it'd be the person's only work. The compliant version: pay a fixed fee per batch of filings completed, let them set their own hours to hit the deadline, put it in writing with a scope doc (this filing type, this volume, this timeframe, here's what's NOT included), collect the W-9 first. Small change in structure, real difference in legal exposure.

Ties forward: Module 23 (bookkeeping/taxes for a sole proprietor) picks up right where this leaves off — once a 1099 exists, it has to land somewhere in your books.
