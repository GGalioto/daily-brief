2🎓 Business School — Module 24: Exit & Acquisition Basics

Closes Arc 7 — the last module in the curriculum as currently scoped. This is the module the last four sessions have been building toward: negotiation (21), hiring (22), bookkeeping (23), and now — how a small business actually gets bought, and why undocumented processes quietly tax the sale price.

The core mechanic: a buyer isn't pricing your revenue. They're pricing your risk.

Two businesses can have identical $500K annual revenue and sell for wildly different prices. The gap isn't the top-line number — it's how much of that revenue survives the current owner walking out the door. Fresh research today (multiple 2026 M&A advisory sources — ExitValue.ai, Iconic, Risepreneur, all independently converging on the same numbers) puts a small, owner-dependent consulting or service firm at 2x–4.5x Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) — a profit-plus-owner-comp figure used for small deals — while a firm with the founder removed from day-to-day, documented processes, and 40%+ recurring/retainer revenue reaches 5x–8x+ Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization (EBITDA), a profitability measure used once a business is big enough that buyers look past just cash flow. Same business, same customers — the multiple more than doubles purely on the basis of "does this run without the person selling it."

Why "undocumented process" is a price-killer, not just an inconvenience

A due-diligence framework published today lists the standard red-flag scan buyers run: key-person dependence, undocumented knowledge, single points of failure. Their own line, worth sitting with: "if key workflows live exclusively in the owner's head, you are buying a job, not a business." A separate 2023 buyer survey they cite found 58% of lower-middle-market businesses have revenue meaningfully tied to the owner's personal relationships, and in 44% the owner is literally the only salesperson — no CRM, no pipeline, just one person's phone and network. Buyers don't just discount for this; they restructure the whole deal around it — heavy earnouts (price paid out over 1-3 years contingent on the business surviving the transition), key-person insurance, mandatory transition periods. The knowledge risk is the same shape whether it's a founder or, as the research also flags directly, "the administrative assistant who holds institutional memory" — a role owners "almost never name... initially" when asked to list their own single points of failure.
