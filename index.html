🎓 Business School — Module 19: Cash Flow & Runway

The mix-up this module fixes: "runway" gets talked about like it only applies to Venture Capital (VC)-funded startups burning through a pile of investor cash until it hits zero. For a bootstrapped operation like Corvino Forge LTD — funded by your day job, not a funding round — the concept still matters, but the failure mode is different. You don't run out of a fixed pool. You get squeezed by timing: money owed to you arrives later than money you owe.

Burn rate is simply what goes out the door every month regardless of revenue. Run the actual Corvino Forge number: GoHighLevel (GHL) Starter plan $104.57/mo (with tax), Namecheap hosting $34.88/yr (≈$2.91/mo), plus whatever small tool costs float in and out — call it roughly $110-150/mo all-in right now. That's tiny. Runway, in the VC sense, is cash-on-hand ÷ burn rate = months until zero. But Corvino Forge has no venture cash pool to divide — the "runway" is really "how long can the day-job income comfortably absorb this $110-150/mo before it's felt," and the honest answer today is indefinitely, because the business isn't yet the thing paying the mortgage.

Where it gets real: the risk isn't burn, it's mismatch — a cost lands before the matching revenue does. CashMoney sidesteps this entirely: $50 one-time sale, cash in hand same day as the sale, zero timing gap. Notification & Compliance is the opposite shape on purpose: if Rainbow signs on, there's a real gap between "Forge does the filing work this month" and "the retainer invoice actually clears" — the exact gap every service business has to manage, and the reason the pricing-anchor work already done (~$100-175/filing, still uncommitted) has to account for payment terms, not just the number itself.

The one-sentence version to be able to explain back: burn rate is what you spend each month no matter what; runway is how long you can survive that spend before income catches up; and for a bootstrapped builder the real skill isn't calculating a runway number, it's picking business models (like CashMoney's instant-cash shape) that shrink the gap between spending the effort and collecting the cash.
