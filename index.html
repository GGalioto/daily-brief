2. 🎓 Business School — Module 21: Negotiation Basics

Anchoring, walking away, and why the first number said in a room matters more than most first-time founders think.

Negotiation isn't a debate you win with better arguments — it's a psychological effect called anchoring. The first concrete number spoken in a negotiation pulls the entire rest of the conversation toward it, even when both sides know the number was arbitrary. This is one of the most replicated findings in behavioral economics: people adjust from an anchor, but they never adjust far enough. Say $150/filing first, and the conversation happens near $150. Let the other side say $50 first, and the conversation happens near $50 — even if $50 was a lowball guess, not a real assessment of value.

The three mechanics that matter for you specifically:

  • Anchor high, but inside the range of plausible. An anchor that's obviously absurd (10x market rate) doesn't pull the conversation — it just signals you're not a serious negotiator and resets the whole thing to zero credibility. Your ~$100–175/filing pricing anchor (session-log, triangulated from Harbor Compliance and permit expediters) is exactly this kind of number: high enough to leave room, grounded enough to survive a raised eyebrow.
  • Whoever needs the deal less controls the anchor. This is why your product-not-ready rule (no pricing until the product is done) is also, structurally, a negotiation tactic even though nobody built it that way on purpose. Terri and Jon have a genuine, worsening problem (Cari going to 3 days/week, zero digital records, no delegation) — that's leverage sitting on your side of the table whether you use it or not.
  • "Walking away" only works if it's real, not performed. You don't need to fake indifference to Rainbow — you're not desperate for this deal (day job covers the bills, Rainbow is one of several lanes) which means you can afford to hold your anchor instead of caving to the first counter. That's the actual meaning of a Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (BATNA): the thing you'll do if this deal falls through. Yours is "keep building the other five lanes," which is a strong BATNA.

Worked example, tied to 8/6: if Jon opens with "what would this cost us," the anchoring mistake is answering with a number you haven't priced yet, live, in the room. The disciplined move — and the one that matches your own product-not-ready rule — is naming the range you've already triangulated ($100–175/filing) as a planning number while being explicit that a real quote needs to know Rainbow's actual volume first (still an open question on your Terri list). That's not stalling — it's controlling the anchor and the information asymmetry at the same time, because right now you know more about pricing this category than Rainbow does.
