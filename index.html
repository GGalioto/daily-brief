2. 🎓 Business School — Module 32: The First Invoice

What actually has to be true before you send an invoice: scope confirmed in writing, payment terms agreed, kickoff date set. All three — not two out of three. Skipping any one of them is how a first client relationship turns into a dispute six weeks in.

Payment terms — the part almost everyone gets sloppy on

Fresh 2026 research on small-business invoicing is blunt about the six most common mistakes: setting no term at all, using ambiguous wording, omitting the late-payment penalty, switching terms mid-engagement, failing to enforce the terms you set, and choosing terms that don't match your own cash-flow reality. The 2026 correction on the classic "Net 30": don't just write "Net 30" — write the actual due date. Net 30 as a phrase invites a dispute about which day it means; a calendar date doesn't.

For a brand-new client (which Rainbow would be, the moment this closes), the standard advice is to run shorter and safer than Net 30 by default: Net 7 or due-on-receipt, plus a deposit if the engagement is sized to justify one. You extend Net 30 to people who've already paid you once, not to people who haven't yet.

Why "scope confirmed in writing" comes first, not last

Payment terms only mean something once both sides agree on what's actually being paid for. That's the connective tissue back to Module 31's pilot framing — a scoped, dated pilot with a named review point isn't just a sales tactic, it's the exact document that makes "what did we agree to" a non-issue when invoice time comes.
