2. 🎓 Business School — Module 20: Willingness To Pay vs. Interest

This closes the original 20-module arc that started 2026-07-06. New modules get appended after this one — the curriculum keeps going, it just isn't a fixed syllabus anymore.

The gap: "interest" is free for the other person to give you. A like, a "that's cool," a nod at a demo, an unprompted comment about cost — none of it has required them to give anything up. Willingness To Pay (WTP) is different in kind, not degree: it's the moment someone trades real money, meaning they're choosing your thing over something else their budget could have bought instead. Founders blow enormous amounts of time and money because they mistake the first for the second.

The cheapest way to test it: don't build more — ask for a commitment before the thing is finished. A deposit, a pre-order, a signed letter of intent, or simply naming a real number out loud and watching whether the other person engages with the number or changes the subject. All four cost you nothing but a conversation.

Your own live example, right now: when you pressed Terri on real interest, her first reaction was cost — and her husband Brian volunteered, unprompted, that your service would probably be "cheaper than paying [the office manager] for 3 days a week." That is a genuinely better signal than a survey answer or a polite "sounds useful" — it was self-generated, it was framed in dollars, and nobody had to ask for it. But it is still not proof of Willingness To Pay. It's proof of interest with a price tag loosely attached. The actual test — the one that would move this from "promising conversation" to "validated" — is naming your real number in a real conversation and watching what happens next: does she engage with it, negotiate it, or go quiet? That's module 20's lesson applied directly to the lane sitting at the top of this brief: the warmest lead in your whole portfolio still hasn't cleared the one bar that actually matters.
