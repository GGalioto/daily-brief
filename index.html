2🎓 Business School — Module 27: Span of Control

The idea: span of control is the classic management-theory question — how many moving parts can one person actually supervise before quality silently degrades? For routine, independent tasks, a manager can reasonably oversee 8–10 people. For non-routine, interdependent work where the output isn't self-evidently checkable (which describes almost everything you delegate to Code and Huginn), that number collapses fast — often to 3 or 4 — because verifying the work takes almost as much judgment as doing it did.

Tonight's session is a textbook demonstration, not a hypothetical. Your current org chart is one person — you — supervising three agents (Corvus, Code, Huginn) producing output on a technically dense, six-track regulatory engine. Over the last ten days, span of control was quietly exceeded more than once, and the tell was exactly what the theory predicts: a gap between what an agent claimed and what was actually true sat undetected until someone did the arithmetic. The validation layer was marked "implemented" on July 30. It sat that way for five days before you ran it directly yourself on August 4 and found two real bugs the claim had missed. Separately, Contact-GUI silently lost its sites table and a systemd (Linux's standard way of keeping a program running in the background, surviving reboots) persistence requirement somewhere across a Huginn-to-Code-to-Huginn handoff chain — and two separate review passes (Huginn's own self-review, then Code's QA pass) both missed it, because both were scoped to "does this look right," never "does this still match what was originally asked for."

The sharpest version of the pattern: Huginn's own self-check has now failed to catch its own math error twice — once on July 29, again last night, both times on the exact same category of mistake (an unaccounted-for fee rule, a misremembered threshold). That's a span-of-control failure one level down — Huginn is one supervisor trying to grade its own 55,000-scenario homework with no second reader.

The fix span-of-control theory actually recommends isn't "pay closer attention." One person's attention doesn't scale by trying harder — it scales by adding structure: narrower checklists, standardized handoffs, and — the piece that visibly worked last night — an independent verifier who is not the same party that did the original work. That's exactly why Code's own independent stress test caught three bugs your direct test missed, and why splitting the swarm into six narrow specialized workers (each testing one thing, not everything) caught six more that one broad pass never would have. The lever isn't watching harder. It's narrowing what any single check is responsible for.
